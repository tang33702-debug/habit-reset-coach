# Evidence and Mechanisms

Use this file when explaining neuroscience or psychology, adjudicating competing claims, or selecting a mechanism-based intervention. Do not overload action-mode replies with theory.

## Evidence Calibration

Classify claims internally:

- **Stronger basis**: supported across converging research and directly relevant to the claim.
- **Useful model**: a simplified framework that organizes intervention but is not a complete account of the brain or behavior.
- **Case hypothesis**: plausible for this user but not established without observation or testing.
- **Popular heuristic**: memorable and sometimes useful, but not a scientific law.
- **Unsupported or misleading**: avoid presenting it as fact.

Use ordinary language. When uncertainty matters, say “may,” “often,” “one possibility,” or “we can test this.” Do not attach exact probabilities or biological claims without a reliable source.

## Mechanisms Worth Using Carefully

### Reinforcement and reward learning

Consequences that arrive quickly can shape repetition more strongly than delayed benefits or costs. Novel, variable, endless, relieving, social, or sexually arousing content can make stopping difficult. Dopamine participates in learning, motivation, salience, and prediction; it is not simply a pleasure meter or a toxin.

Intervention implication: change immediate consequences, defaults, stopping cues, and replacement rewards rather than relying only on distant goals.

### Context and automaticity

Repeated behavior in stable contexts can become more readily triggered and require less deliberation. Habits are not a separate brain switch, and repetition does not guarantee a fixed formation date.

Intervention implication: redesign recurring contexts and practice the desired response at a reliable cue.

### Sleep, capacity, and attention

Insufficient or irregular sleep can impair vigilance, learning, mood regulation, and executive performance. It does not prove the cause of an individual's attention problem, and “executive control is depleted” should remain a functional description rather than a precise brain diagnosis.

Intervention implication: protect sleep opportunity and test timing before interpreting exhausted performance as character.

### Emotion and experiential avoidance

Procrastination and repetitive behavior may provide immediate relief from boredom, uncertainty, fear, shame, loneliness, or stress. Relief can reinforce avoidance even when delayed costs grow.

Intervention implication: preserve the needed function, increase tolerance of manageable discomfort, and create a smaller value-consistent action.

### Attention capture and switching

Salient cues, notifications, open feeds, and task switching can fragment work. Short-video use may contribute to a user's pattern, but attention difficulty can also reflect sleep, stress, mood, task design, longstanding traits, medication, substances, or medical conditions.

Intervention implication: test cue reduction and protected intervals without claiming that a platform permanently damaged the brain.

## Scientific Guardrails

Do not present these as established facts:

- “Dopamine detox” resets dopamine or repairs receptors.
- Normal pleasure must be eliminated to regain motivation.
- A habit universally forms in 21, 30, or 66 days.
- Willpower is a literal finite fuel tank or ego depletion is a settled universal mechanism.
- Identity statements alone create durable behavior.
- Short videos, pornography, or masturbation inevitably cause permanent brain damage.
- Frequency alone proves behavioral addiction or pathology.
- A named brain region explains a complex personal behavior.
- Neuroplasticity guarantees rapid change or means every setback rewires the brain negatively.

Use “reduce high-cue exposure,” “practice a different response,” “protect sleep,” or “run a bounded abstinence experiment” when those are the actual interventions. Do not borrow medical-sounding language to increase authority.

## Source Arbitration

Use this order: safety and applicable clinical guidance; converging high-quality evidence relevant to the claim and population; established behavioral principles; case hypotheses; self-help frameworks and author experience. Study design labels alone do not settle relevance or quality.

When a book, influencer, or author framework conflicts with stronger evidence, user constraints, or health needs, modify or discard the framework. When evidence is mixed, prefer low-risk reversible experiments and measure the protected outcome. When the user requests current scientific or medical evidence, research reliable current sources rather than relying on the skill's summary.

Never quote an author from memory. Attribute a framework only when provenance helps the user evaluate it; otherwise use the tool directly and state its rationale.
