# Practical Framework Toolkit

Use this as a selection library, not as a council of personalities. Choose one primary tool for the current bottleneck and at most one support tool for a different necessary bottleneck. Do not imitate authors, quote from memory, or imply that a bestselling framework has clinical authority.

Before choosing: use live rescue for an active loop; protect health and safety first; clarify goal ownership before optimizing an ambivalent target; and treat direct observations as more informative about this user than a favored framework.

## Visible Cues and Environment Defaults

Associated with practical habit-design writing such as James Clear and research traditions represented by Wendy Wood.

- **Problem**: the intended behavior is forgotten or the competing behavior is the default.
- **Mechanism**: make the desired cue and materials salient; make the unwanted first step slightly less automatic.
- **Use when**: context is recurring and the user can change it.
- **Avoid when**: the main barrier is pain, severe fatigue, fear, unrewarding content, or goal mismatch.
- **Failure signal**: the cue is noticed but action still does not start. Diagnose entry cost, emotion, reward, or autonomy next.

## Tiny Starter and Shaping

Associated with BJ Fogg and practical habit-building frameworks.

- **Problem**: the user endorses the behavior but repeatedly fails at entry.
- **Mechanism**: reduce ability demands and reinforce successful initiation before expanding.
- **Use when**: setup, commitment, or perceived task size blocks starting.
- **Avoid when**: the starter already occurs reliably but continuation fails.
- **Failure signal**: frequent starts with immediate disengagement. Improve content, difficulty, feedback, or session design rather than shrinking again.

## Implementation Intention

Associated with Peter Gollwitzer's research tradition.

- **Problem**: intention exists but is not translated into action at the right moment.
- **Mechanism**: specify a controllable cue and response: when X occurs, do Y.
- **Use when**: X is stable and Y is precise and feasible.
- **Avoid when**: the cue is unpredictable or Y still requires a large decision.
- **Failure signal**: X occurs but Y is repeatedly skipped. Reduce Y or address competing rewards and emotion.

## Immediate Reward or Temptation Bundling

Associated with behavior-change work represented by Katy Milkman.

- **Problem**: benefits are delayed and the activity feels unrewarding.
- **Mechanism**: pair the behavior with an immediate compatible pleasure or reserve a wanted activity for that context.
- **Use when**: the pairing does not impair the target and remains proportionate.
- **Avoid when**: the reward becomes the main distraction, creates health costs, or undermines intrinsic interest.
- **Failure signal**: the user only performs the reward or the bundle reduces quality. Change or remove the pairing.

## Commitment Device and Social Structure

- **Problem**: repeated preference reversals defeat lighter environmental changes.
- **Mechanism**: voluntarily make the future unwanted choice harder or make the intended action socially concrete.
- **Use when**: the user understands the tradeoff and freely agrees to a reversible, proportionate constraint.
- **Avoid when**: coercion, humiliation, secrecy, financial danger, relationship control, or health risk is involved.
- **Failure signal**: evasion, resentment, concealment, or escalating punishment. Reduce control and reassess ownership or function.

## Protected Focus Block

Associated with Cal Newport's practical deep-work framework and broader attention-management principles.

- **Problem**: the user can start but interruptions and switching prevent sustained work.
- **Mechanism**: define one outcome, a finite interval, a low-interruption surface, and a clear finish.
- **Use when**: task entry already works and a protected interval is realistic.
- **Avoid when**: the user cannot yet start, is severely sleep deprived, or the work requires responsiveness.
- **Failure signal**: the block is repeatedly avoided or collapses early. Shorten it, clarify the output, or diagnose difficulty and fear.

## Identity Framing

Popularized in books such as *Atomic Habits*.

- **Problem**: the user wants a meaningful narrative that supports repetition.
- **Mechanism**: treat small actions as evidence of a chosen direction, not proof of a fixed trait.
- **Use when**: identity language feels freely chosen and forgiving.
- **Avoid when**: it creates purity, shame, perfectionism, or “one lapse disproves who I am.”
- **Failure signal**: self-labeling becomes harsher after a miss. Return to behavior-specific language and recovery.

## Function-Matched Replacement

- **Problem**: removing a behavior leaves boredom, relief, connection, rest, movement, or sexual tension unmet.
- **Mechanism**: offer a lower-cost response that supplies part of the same immediate function.
- **Use when**: the function is reasonably observed or testable.
- **Avoid when**: the replacement is moralistic, unrealistic, or unrelated to the need.
- **Failure signal**: the user consistently ignores it. Reassess the actual reward or make the alternative easier and more immediate.

## Arbitration Rule

Do not stack these tools by default. Select the tool that directly matches the diagnosed failure. If it fails, use the failure signal to update the hypothesis; do not add every remaining tool at once.
