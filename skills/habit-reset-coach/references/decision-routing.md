# Diagnostic Routing

Use this file during calm diagnosis, prevention, or repeated failure. In a live loop, use the rescue protocol first and diagnose later.

## Route by Failure Topology

Choose the first branch that explains the main breakdown. Multiple causes may exist, but select one primary target for the first experiment.

### 1. The cue is absent or forgotten

Evidence: the intended action rarely enters awareness at the relevant time.

Test stable anchors, visible materials, calendar or location cues, or a simpler default. Do not solve a missing cue with motivation speeches.

### 2. The user remembers but cannot start

Distinguish:

- **Unclear next action**: define the first visible physical action.
- **Entry cost is too high**: reduce setup, size, or commitment.
- **Low capacity**: move the task, protect sleep or food, or lower cognitive demand.
- **Fear, shame, or evaluation**: use an intentionally rough private first pass and psychological tools.
- **A closer reward wins**: change defaults or add small friction to the competitor.
- **The goal is not endorsed**: clarify ownership and consequences before designing compliance.

Use a tiny starter only when entry cost is the likely bottleneck.

### 3. The user starts but cannot sustain

Evidence: entry happens, then engagement collapses or repeatedly fragments.

Check difficulty mismatch, unclear finish line, slow feedback, interruptions, physical discomfort, unrewarding material, or unrealistic session length. Improve the work surface, feedback, challenge, breaks, or content. Do not keep shrinking the starter when the starter already works.

### 4. The user starts an unwanted behavior but cannot stop

Evidence: the main loss of control occurs after entry.

Check variable or endless rewards, autoplay, absence of stopping cues, fatigue, emotional relief, social reinforcement, or arousal. Use the rescue ladder during the episode; in calm setup, add stopping cues, finite units, reversible friction, location changes, or function-matched alternatives. A small desired-habit starter alone does not solve an endless reward loop.

### 5. A lapse becomes a cascade

Evidence: one miss or episode triggers “already ruined,” shame, compensatory rules, or further repetition.

Protect the next block, reject compensation, restore at the next cue, and examine all-or-nothing rules. Measure recovery time rather than only frequency.

### 6. The goal conflicts with values or reality

Evidence: the target is mainly imposed by comparison, shame, another person's preference, or an idealized identity; the expected benefit is vague; repeated resistance remains even when the behavior is easy and rewarding.

Use values clarification and decisional balance. The correct outcome may be modify, reduce frequency, substitute, postpone, or drop the goal. Do not optimize obedience to an unowned target.

Distinguish an unowned goal from an instrumental task. The user may dislike a required course, application, or work task while freely valuing graduation, income, craft, or responsibility. In that case, connect the task to the higher-level value and improve its design; do not require the user to enjoy it.

## Select the Maintaining Domain

Use observed context to choose a leading hypothesis:

- **Capacity**: inadequate sleep opportunity, irregular sleep, hunger, illness, pain, medication or substance effects, sustained overload.
- **Cue and environment**: salient prompts, default access, location, social context, or missing materials.
- **Task design**: ambiguity, size, difficulty, delayed feedback, no stopping point.
- **Reward and learning**: immediate stimulation, relief, novelty, arousal, or social reward outweighs delayed benefits.
- **Emotion regulation**: the behavior reduces boredom, anxiety, loneliness, shame, anger, or fear in the short term.
- **Belief and rule**: perfectionism, catastrophizing, “all or nothing,” or waiting for motivation.
- **Value and autonomy**: weak ownership, conflicting priorities, reactance, or an imagined rather than useful identity.

Do not infer a hidden cause solely from the behavior. Phrase it as: “One possibility is X; if so, we would expect Y. Let us test Z.”

## One-Question Rule

Ask a question only when different answers produce different interventions. Prefer questions about the most recent concrete episode:

- “Did you forget, or remember and avoid starting?”
- “When you stopped, was it because it felt hard, boring, interrupted, or physically tiring?”
- “Before opening the app, were you seeking stimulation, relief, rest, connection, or acting automatically?”
- “If this became effortless, would you actually want it?”

Avoid long intake interviews. If uncertainty remains, choose a safe reversible test that separates two plausible explanations.

## Escalation Logic

Escalate the intervention only when a lighter, correctly targeted intervention was actually tested and the user agrees. Change the hypothesis rather than merely increasing restriction when results contradict it.

- If the intervention was not executed, shrink it, move it earlier, or choose a different entry; this does not test the maintaining hypothesis.
- If it was executed but the predicted intermediate change did not occur, replace the hypothesis or mechanism instead of stacking rules.
- If partial improvement occurred, preserve the useful element and change only one variable.
- If shame, rebound, sleep loss, concealment, or other harm increases, stop and use a lower-cost approach.

Escalate toward professional assessment when health warning signs, persistent cross-setting attention problems, severe distress, injury, danger, or substantial impairment exceed the scope of habit coaching.
